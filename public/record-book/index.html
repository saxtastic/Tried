CL-1857-DRED-SCOTT

Closure of the federal forum to persons of African descent

6 March 1857 · Washington, District of Columbia

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
No investigation was ever opened

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

Not a violent act. The entry records a lawful judicial holding that operated as the precondition for the violence in every entry below. It is carried here because a log that recorded only the blows and not the rule that made them unanswerable would be incomplete.

Conduct

The Court held that persons of African descent, whether enslaved or free, were not and could not become citizens within the meaning of Article III, and therefore could not invoke the jurisdiction of the federal courts; and further that Congress lacked power to prohibit slavery in the territories.

Harmed

Dred Scott, Harriet Robinson Scott

Count: 2 documented

Collective: Persons of African descent within the United States

Interstitial harm

Every injury suffered by a person of African descent between 1857 and 1868 that went unheard because this holding closed the forum. Those injuries have no entries anywhere, and cannot be counted.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Chief Justice Roger B. Taney and the concurring majority, acting in judicial capacity

Process and disposition

Forum: Supreme Court of the United States

No process was possible, because the holding was itself the act complained of. It is entered first in this book because it establishes the condition every earlier complaint was decided under and every later one had to overcome: not that the injury was excused, but that no forum would hear it.

Impediments

Denial of legal personhood

Remedy

Granted

  • Superseded by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments and the Civil Rights Act of 1866

Outstanding

The decision was never overruled by the Court; it was displaced by constitutional amendment.

Citation: Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393 (1857).

Sources: SRC-DLAS

CL-CHATTEL-ENSLAVEMENT

Chattel enslavement in the United States and its colonial predecessors

1619 – 6 December 1865 · The slaveholding colonies and states, and the domestic trade between them

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
No investigation was ever opened

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

enslavementforced labourrapekidnappinghomicidemayhemfamily separation

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

Holding of persons as property; compelled uncompensated labor; sale and separation of families; sexual violence without cognizable offense; killing and maiming under statutes that graded such acts as damage to property or excused them entirely; and denial of the legal capacity to testify, contract, marry, or sue.

Harmed

Enslaved persons in the United States and its colonial predecessors, Frederick Douglass, Harriet Ann Jacobs, Sojourner Truth, Solomon Northup

Count: estimated 3,900,000–4,000,000 — Approximately 3.9 million persons were enslaved in the United States at the 1860 census. The number of persons enslaved across the full period is larger by an order that this book does not estimate.

Collective: Enslaved persons in North America and their descendants

Interstitial harm

Wealth transferred and still held. Names not preserved and now unrecoverable. Kinship lines severed by sale, which is why descendants of this entry frequently cannot document the ancestry that a claim would require of them.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Slaveholders; slave traders; sheriffs, patrols, and courts enforcing the slave codes; legislatures enacting them; insurers, banks, and shippers financing the trade

Process and disposition

There was no forum. The conduct was lawful where it occurred and its victims lacked the legal capacity to complain of it. This entry exists because a log of unpunished crimes that omitted it on the ground that it was not then a crime would be reproducing the impediment rather than recording it.

Impediments

Denial of legal personhoodStatute of limitationsAbsence of descendant standing

Remedy

Sought

  • General Orders No. 15 (Special Field Orders No. 15), 1865, rescinded the same year
  • H.R. 40, introduced in every Congress since 1989, never enacted

Granted

  • Abolition by the Thirteenth Amendment, which is not compensation

Outstanding

No compensation was ever paid to any enslaved person by the United States. Compensation was paid to slaveholders in the District of Columbia under the Compensated Emancipation Act of 1862.

The register cannot be completed for this entry and never will be. Named individuals appear where a narrative, register, petition, or bill of sale preserved a name; the overwhelming majority did not. See PR-COLLECTIVE-ENSLAVED-US.

CL-1841-NORTHUP

Kidnapping of Solomon Northup and the failure of the prosecution

April 1841 – 3 January 1853 · Washington, District of Columbia

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
Charges filed; acquittal

Unpunished record: documented

kidnappingfalse imprisonmentenslavementforced labour

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

Solomon Northup, a free citizen of New York, was drugged, seized, held in a slave pen within sight of the Capitol, and sold south. He was held in slavery for twelve years until his identity was established and he was recovered by an agent of the Governor of New York.

Harmed

Solomon Northup

Count: 1 documented

Collective: Free Black citizens of the northern states, of whom an unknown number were kidnapped and sold and never recovered

Interstitial harm

Twelve years of a life. An unknown number of other free Black northerners kidnapped in the same trade who were never recovered and so left no narrative, no prosecution, and no entry.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

James H. Birch, the trader who sold him in Washington; Alexander Merrill and Joseph Russell, charged in New York with the kidnapping

Process and disposition

Forum: Criminal court, District of Columbia (1853); Saratoga and Washington County courts, New York (1854-1857)

Birch was charged in Washington and acquitted. The reason is the whole of this entry: Northup, the only witness to his own kidnapping, was barred by District law from testifying against a white man. Birch was permitted to testify in his own defence. The subsequent New York prosecution of Merrill and Russell was obstructed for years by contests over jurisdiction between the two states and ended without a conviction.

Impediments

Denial of legal personhoodExclusion of Black jurors

Remedy

Sought

  • Criminal prosecution in the District of Columbia, 1853
  • Criminal prosecution in New York, 1854-1857

Granted

  • Recovery of his liberty, by executive action of the Governor of New York under an 1840 statute

Outstanding

No conviction. No compensation for twelve years of uncompensated labour. Northup's own date and place of death are unknown.

Entered as the cleanest single illustration of the difference this book records. The crime was documented, the perpetrator was named, the forum was open, and the victim was silenced by a rule of evidence. Twelve Years a Slave (1853) is the complaint he filed instead, and it is the one that survived.

Sources: SRC-DOCSOUTH, SRC-CHRONICLING-AMERICA

CL-1873-COLFAX

Colfax massacre and the reversal of the convictions

13 April 1873 · Colfax, Grant Parish County, Louisiana

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
Convictions obtained, then set aside

Unpunished record: documented

mass homicidehomicide after surrender

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

An armed white force attacked Black state militia and freedmen defending the Grant Parish courthouse following the disputed 1872 Louisiana election, and killed a large number of them, most after they had surrendered.

Harmed

Black residents of Grant Parish killed at Colfax

Count: estimated 60–150 — Counts vary by source. Most of the dead were killed after surrender.

Collective: Black residents of Grant Parish

Interstitial harm

The collapse of Reconstruction enforcement in Louisiana. Cruikshank's holding then travelled outward from these dead and disposed of prosecutions in states that had nothing to do with Grant Parish.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of the attacking force, including former Confederate soldiers and members of the White League

Process and disposition

Forum: United States Circuit Court, District of Louisiana; Supreme Court of the United States

Federal prosecutions under the Enforcement Act of 1870 produced three convictions. In United States v. Cruikshank, 92 U.S. 542 (1876), the Supreme Court set them aside, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment reached only state action and that the indictment failed to charge a federal offense. No one was ever punished for the killings.

Impediments

State action doctrineExclusion of Black jurors

Remedy

Outstanding

None sought or obtained. A Louisiana state marker erected in 1950 described the event as a riot and was removed in 2021.

The controlling entry for the state action impediment: the case that created the doctrine is the case in which the only convictions were lost.

Conduct

Three Black grocers, held in the Shelby County jail after a confrontation arising from white competitors' hostility to their store, were taken from the jail by a mob and killed. The competing grocer's business interest in eliminating the People's Grocery is documented as the motive.

Harmed

Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, William Stewart

Count: 3 documented

Collective: The Black business community of the Curve, Memphis

Interstitial harm

The People's Grocery was sold at a fraction of its value to the competitor whose hostility produced the killings. Several thousand Black residents left Memphis. Neither the transfer nor the exodus appears in any register of loss.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of the mob; the sheriff and jailers who surrendered custody of prisoners in their charge

Process and disposition

Forum: Shelby County

No one was charged. Prisoners in lawful state custody were handed over to a mob by the officers responsible for them, which is the paradigm case of an official acting beyond the authority of the office.

Impediments

Coroner's finding of death at the hands of persons unknownExclusion of Black jurorsState action doctrine

Remedy

Outstanding

None. The killings caused a mass departure of Black residents from Memphis and began Ida B. Wells's investigative work.

Wells was godmother to Thomas Moss's daughter. Her investigation of these killings produced the analytical method this record book uses.

CL-1918-TURNER

Killing of Mary Turner and the Brooks-Lowndes lynchings

17 May 1918 – 24 May 1918 · Barney, near the Little River, Lowndes and Brooks County, Georgia

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
No investigation was ever opened

Unpunished record: documented

homicidetorturekilling of an unborn child

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

During a week of lynchings following the killing of a plantation owner, Mary Turner publicly denounced the killing of her husband Hayes Turner and said she would seek warrants against those responsible. A mob seized her the next day and killed her. She was in the eighth month of pregnancy; her unborn child was killed as well.

Harmed

Mary Turner, Hayes Turner, Name unrecorded - the unborn child of Mary Turner, Persons killed in the Brooks and Lowndes County lynchings, May 1918

Count: 11 documented — At least eleven people were killed over the week; sources differ on the total.

Collective: Black residents of Brooks and Lowndes Counties, several hundred of whom left the area

Interstitial harm

Several hundred Black residents left Brooks and Lowndes Counties in the following weeks, abandoning crops mid-season. Mary Turner's stated intention to swear out warrants is the last recorded attempt by anyone to use a court in that county for these killings.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of the mob. Walter White's investigation for the NAACP obtained the names of participants and transmitted them to the Governor of Georgia.

Process and disposition

Forum: Brooks County; Lowndes County; Georgia

No one was charged, notwithstanding that the NAACP delivered a list of named participants to the state. The Department of Justice declined to act. This entry is the clearest demonstration that the impediment was not the difficulty of identification.

Impediments

Coroner's finding of death at the hands of persons unknownExclusion of Black jurorsState action doctrine

Remedy

Granted

  • Georgia historical marker, 2010, later repeatedly vandalized and removed in 2020

Outstanding

None.

Mary Turner was killed for announcing an intention to use the courts. The record book notes this without comment.

CL-1919-ELAINE

Elaine massacre and the capital convictions of the survivors

30 September 1919 – 7 October 1919 · Elaine and Hoop Spur, Phillips County, Arkansas

Act
established
Actor
described unidentified
Forum
Convictions obtained, then set aside

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

mass homicidetorture to obtain confessionmalicious prosecution

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

Black sharecroppers meeting to organize the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America were attacked; posses and federal troops killed a large number of Black residents across the county over several days. Survivors were arrested, and confessions were obtained by torture.

Harmed

Frank Moore, Black residents of Phillips County killed, September-October 1919

Count: estimated 100–240 — No official count was made. Five white men also died.

Collective: Black sharecroppers and residents of Phillips County

Interstitial harm

The union that the meeting was called to form. No coroner's inquest was held for any of the dead and no burial was recorded, so the absence of a count is not a gap in this book's research but a fact about what was done.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of posses drawn from Phillips and surrounding counties; United States Army troops; county officers conducting the interrogations

Process and disposition

Forum: Phillips County Circuit Court; Supreme Court of the United States

No participant in the killings was charged. Instead, 122 Black residents were indicted and twelve were sentenced to death after trials lasting minutes before all-white juries surrounded by a mob. In Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), the Supreme Court held that a trial dominated by mob pressure denies due process, and the convictions were eventually undone. The outcome recorded here is the vacatur of the victims' convictions; no perpetrator was ever tried.

Impediments

Exclusion of Black jurorsState action doctrineStatute of limitations

Remedy

Granted

  • Release of the twelve condemned men by 1925
  • Moore v. Dempsey established federal habeas review of mob-dominated trials

Outstanding

No compensation to any victim or descendant. No count of the dead was ever made.

Records the inversion this book exists to document: the survivors were the only people prosecuted.

Sources: SRC-MOORE-DEMPSEY, SRC-NAACP-PAPERS, SRC-CHRONICLING-AMERICA

CL-1921-TULSA

Destruction of the Greenwood District, Tulsa

31 May 1921 – 1 June 1921 · Tulsa, Greenwood District, Tulsa County, Oklahoma

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
No investigation was ever opened

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

mass homicidearsonmass unlawful detentiondestruction of property

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

A white mob, including men deputized by city officials and armed from public and private stocks, destroyed the thirty-five block Greenwood District, killed residents, and detained thousands of survivors in internment camps. Aircraft were used over the district.

Harmed

Residents and property owners of the Greenwood District

Count: 39 confirmed; estimates to 300 — The 2001 Commission recorded 39 confirmed deaths and reviewed estimates ranging to 300. More than 1,250 homes were destroyed and roughly 6,000 people were detained.

Collective: Residents and property owners of the Greenwood District

Interstitial harm

Insurance denied under riot clauses, converting destruction into total uncompensated loss. Deeds and records burned with the district, so the proof of ownership that a later claim would require was destroyed by the same act that created the claim.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of the mob; city officials who deputized them and issued arms; National Guard units

Process and disposition

Forum: Tulsa County; Oklahoma; United States District Court, Northern District of Oklahoma

No one was convicted of any killing or of the destruction. A grand jury convened in 1921 attributed the cause to Black residents. Federal litigation by survivors was dismissed as time-barred and certiorari was denied in Alexander v. Oklahoma, 382 F.3d 1206 (10th Cir. 2004). A later state public nuisance action brought by survivors was dismissed by the Oklahoma Supreme Court in 2024.

Impediments

Exclusion of Black jurorsStatute of limitationsAbsence of descendant standingRiot exclusion in property insuranceSovereign and qualified immunity

Remedy

Sought

  • Compensation recommended by the Oklahoma Commission, 2001
  • Alexander v. Oklahoma
  • Oklahoma public nuisance action, dismissed 2024

Granted

  • State commission report and scholarships; no direct compensation to survivors or descendants

Outstanding

Property losses were never compensated by insurers or by the state. Survivors of the massacre were still living when the 2024 dismissal was entered.

The entry in which every impediment in this book operates at once: no prosecution, no insurance, no standing, no timeliness, no immunity waiver.

Sources: SRC-TULSA-COMMISSION, SRC-CHRONICLING-AMERICA

CL-1923-ROSEWOOD

Destruction of Rosewood, Florida

1 January 1923 – 7 January 1923 · Rosewood, Levy County, Florida

Act

Forum

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

homicidearsonforcible displacement

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

Following an allegation by a white woman in nearby Sumner, a white mob attacked the Black town of Rosewood over several days, killed residents, and burned the town. Survivors fled into the swamps and the town ceased to exist.

Harmed

Sarah Carrier, Sylvester Carrier, Residents and landowners of Rosewood

Collective: The residents and landowners of Rosewood

Interstitial harm

A town that ceased to exist. Survivors dispersed under conditions that made them unlocatable for seventy years, which is why the 1994 compensation reached so few of them.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Members of the mob drawn from surrounding counties; the Levy County sheriff, who declined to intervene

Process and disposition

Forum: Levy County; Florida Legislature

A 1923 grand jury returned no indictments. No one was ever prosecuted. Seventy-one years later the Florida Legislature enacted the Rosewood Compensation Act of 1994, providing payments to survivors, a property-loss claims process, and a scholarship fund for descendants.

Impediments

Exclusion of Black jurorsStatute of limitations

Remedy

Sought

  • Claim bill before the Florida Legislature, 1993-1994

Granted

  • Rosewood Compensation Act of 1994: payments to survivors, a property claims process, and the Rosewood Family Scholarship Fund

Outstanding

No criminal accountability. Compensation was partial and reached a small number of claimants.

The precedent entry. Rosewood is the clearest American instance of legislative reparation for a specific massacre, and it demonstrates the route that Tulsa litigation could not take. Note the outcome value: legislative_remedy is still unpunished, because no perpetrator was tried.

CL-1932-TUSKEGEE-STUDY

United States Public Health Service Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee

1932 – November 1972 · Tuskegee, Macon County, Alabama

Act
established
Actor
named in sources
Forum
Money paid; no criminal accountability

Unpunished record: documented Acting under colour of law

fraudbatterynon consensual human experimentationdeprivation of treatment

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

The Public Health Service enrolled approximately 600 Black men, 399 with latent syphilis, under the representation that they were being treated for bad blood. They were not treated. When penicillin became the standard of care in the 1940s, the Service acted to keep participants from receiving it, including through arrangements with local physicians and draft boards, in order to observe the untreated course of the disease to autopsy.

Harmed

Men enrolled in the United States Public Health Service study at Tuskegee, and their families

Count: 623 documented — 399 men with syphilis and 201 controls. Wives and children were infected in consequence; the exact number was never established.

Collective: The enrolled men, their wives, and children born with congenital syphilis

Interstitial harm

Wives infected and children born with congenital syphilis, never fully enumerated. A documented and persisting withdrawal of trust in public medicine among Black Americans, which is a harm to people who were never enrolled.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Officers and physicians of the United States Public Health Service, acting in official capacity over forty years

Process and disposition

Forum: United States District Court, Middle District of Alabama; congressional oversight

No one was criminally prosecuted. The study ended in 1972 after press disclosure. A class action settled in 1974 for approximately $10 million with lifetime medical benefits for participants and affected family members. A presidential apology followed in 1997.

Impediments

Sovereign and qualified immunityStatute of limitations

Remedy

Sought

  • Class action, 1973

Granted

  • 1974 settlement, approximately $10 million
  • Tuskegee Health Benefit Program
  • Presidential apology, 16 May 1997

Outstanding

No criminal accountability for any officer. The study prompted the National Research Act of 1974 and the Belmont Report.

Source id is the Tuskegee University Archives generally; the study records are held by NARA Southeast and the CDC. Entered as the clearest instance of officials acting beyond the statute of their role over a forty-year span with no criminal consequence.

Sources: SRC-TUSKEGEE-LYNCHING

CL-1949-GROVELAND

Groveland Four

16 July 1949 – 6 November 1951 · Groveland and Lake County, Lake County, Florida

Offences named characterise the conduct. Naming an offence is not naming an offender: that is the Actor axis above.

Conduct

Four young Black men were accused of rape on evidence later found to be without foundation. Ernest Thomas was pursued and killed by a posse. The three others were beaten in custody to obtain confessions. After the Supreme Court reversed the convictions in Shepherd v. Florida, 341 U.S. 50 (1951), Sheriff Willis McCall shot Samuel Shepherd and Walter Irvin while transporting them in handcuffs; Shepherd died and Irvin survived and contradicted the sheriff's account.

Harmed

Ernest Thomas, Samuel Shepherd, Walter Irvin, Charles Greenlee

Count: 4 documented

Interstitial harm

Sheriff McCall remained in office for twenty-one years after the shooting and the deaths in his custody during that period are separately documented. Charles Greenlee lived to be refused clemency repeatedly and died seven years before the pardons issued.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Sheriff Willis V. McCall and deputies of Lake County, acting under color of law

Process and disposition

Forum: Lake County Circuit Court; Supreme Court of the United States; Florida Clemency Board; Lake County Circuit Court (2021)

A coroner's inquest cleared McCall of the shooting; he was never prosecuted and remained sheriff until 1972. Irvin was retried, convicted again, and later paroled. Florida issued posthumous pardons in 2019, and in 2021 a court formally exonerated all four and dismissed the indictments. No officer was ever charged for the shooting or for the beatings.

Impediments

Exclusion of Black jurorsSovereign and qualified immunityStatute of limitationsSeparate but equal

Remedy

Granted

  • Posthumous pardons, 2019
  • Judicial exoneration and dismissal of indictments, 2021
  • Florida claim bill compensating the families, 2023

Outstanding

No criminal accountability for any officer. Exoneration corrected the state's injury to the victims and left the perpetrators' conduct unaddressed.

Outcome is recorded as posthumous_exoneration and therefore still counts as unpunished. See METHODOLOGY.md section 5 for why.

Conduct

A fourteen-year-old boy visiting from Chicago was taken from his great-uncle's house at night, beaten, shot, and sunk in the Tallahatchie River, following an allegation about an encounter at a store in Money.

Harmed

Emmett Louis Till, Mamie Till-Mobley

Count: 1 documented

Interstitial harm

Bryant and Milam were paid for the confession. Mamie Till-Mobley spent the remaining forty-eight years of her life producing the record that the acquittal had refused to produce.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Roy Bryant and J.W. Milam, tried and acquitted; other participants named in later scholarship were never charged

Process and disposition

Forum: Circuit Court of Tallahatchie County; federal grand jury, 2006-2007; DOJ review closed 2021

An all-white, all-male jury acquitted Bryant and Milam after deliberating slightly over an hour. Months later, protected by double jeopardy, both described committing the killing in a paid interview published in Look magazine. The Department of Justice reopened the matter twice and closed it in December 2021 without charges.

Impediments

Exclusion of Black jurorsLapse of federal cold-case jurisdictionStatute of limitationsSeparate but equal

Remedy

Granted

  • Emmett Till Unsolved Civil Rights Crime Act of 2007
  • Emmett Till Antilynching Act, Pub. L. 117-107 (2022), making lynching a federal hate crime
  • Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National Monument, 2023

Outstanding

No one was ever punished. Mamie Till-Mobley's decision to hold an open-casket funeral, and to permit the photographs to be published, is the reason the record exists at all.

The confession is documented and the acquittal is final. This is the entry that demonstrates that in this book unpunished never means unproven.

CL-1960-KENNARD

Framing and imprisonment of Clyde Kennard

Conduct

After Clyde Kennard applied repeatedly to enroll at Mississippi Southern College, state officials, acting through the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, arranged his prosecution for accessory to the theft of chicken feed valued at twenty-five dollars. He was sentenced to seven years at hard labor. He developed colon cancer in prison and was denied adequate care.

Harmed

Clyde Kennard

Count: 1 documented

Interstitial harm

The degree awarded to nobody. The Sovereignty Commission operated for a further seventeen years on legislative appropriation, and its files record other arrangements of the same kind.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Officials of the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, a state agency, and cooperating local law enforcement and prosecutors

Process and disposition

Forum: Forrest County Circuit Court; Forrest County Circuit Court (2006)

The conviction was vacated in 2006 after the sole witness recanted and the Sovereignty Commission's own files, opened by court order in 1998, documented the arrangement. Kennard died in 1963. No official was ever prosecuted for procuring the conviction.

Impediments

Sovereign and qualified immunityStatute of limitationsExclusion of Black jurors

Remedy

Granted

  • Vacatur of conviction, 2006

Outstanding

No accountability for any official. The agency that arranged it was a lawfully constituted arm of the State of Mississippi, funded by the legislature.

The Sovereignty Commission files are the rare case where the impediment documented itself. Held by the Mississippi Department of Archives and History.

Sources: SRC-CRRJ, SRC-NRE

Killing of persons by mobs outside any legal process, as enumerated by the NAACP's compilation of press and investigative records over thirty years.

Harmed

Persons recorded as lynched in the United States, 1889-1918

Count: 3,224 documented — 702 of those recorded were white. The compilation is acknowledged by its own authors to undercount, as it depends on press reporting.

Collective: Persons lynched in the United States, 1889-1918

Interstitial harm

The departures, the crops left, the businesses sold under duress, and the testimony never given, none of which any of the three competing tallies attempted to count.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Respondents

Mobs, in the great majority of cases never identified in any official record; officers who surrendered custody in a substantial number of cases

Process and disposition

Forum: The several states

Prosecutions were exceptional and convictions nearly unknown. Federal anti-lynching legislation was introduced repeatedly from 1918 and was defeated by filibuster on every occasion until 2022.

Impediments

Coroner's finding of death at the hands of persons unknownExclusion of Black jurorsState action doctrine

Remedy

  • Approximately 200 subsequent bills, all defeated

Granted

  • Senate resolution of apology, 2005
  • Emmett Till Antilynching Act, 2022

Outstanding

The apology came eighty-seven years after the compilation was published. This entry stands for every killing in it that has no entry of its own.
