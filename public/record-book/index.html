22 entries. Each is read on three independent axes. Act: did the violence occur — answered by bodies, ruins and records, not by a forum. Actor: who did it. Forum: what a court or commission did about it. In 22 entries the act is established on the evidence; in 3 no participant is identified in any source; in 21 no perpetrator was ever convicted. The crime is captured whether or not anyone was ever made to answer for it, because those are not the same question.

A fourth axis records the only resolve that reaches the harmed. 5 of 22 entries produced anything material for the victims; 5 produced nothing at all; none produced full restitution. Punishing an offender was never the remedy for an injury, and this book measures the remedy separately from the punishment so that neither can stand in for the other.

Conduct

The Court held that persons of African descent, whether enslaved or free, were not and could not become citizens within the meaning of Article III, and therefore could not invoke the jurisdiction of the federal courts; and further that Congress lacked power to prohibit slavery in the territories.

Every injury suffered by a person of African descent between 1857 and 1868 that went unheard because this holding closed the forum. Those injuries have no entries anywhere, and cannot be counted.

Conduct

Approximately one million people were bought in the Upper South and transported, in coffles overland and by ship along the coast, to be sold in the Deep South. Sales were transacted at arm's length between strangers, recorded in bills of sale, entered in county deed and probate books, insured, and pledged as collateral for loans. Families were separated as a routine incident of the business: spouses sold apart, children sold from parents.

Harmed

People trafficked in the domestic slave trade, 1808-1865

Count: estimated 800,000–1,200,000 — Approximately one million people forcibly relocated over the period. Estimates of families broken vary; a commonly cited figure is that the trade destroyed something on the order of a third of enslaved marriages in the exporting states, and separated a comparable share of children from a parent. The book records the divergence rather than a single number.

Collective: Enslaved people of the Upper South and the families left behind

Interstitial harm

The letters written to find a sold spouse or child, and the advertisements placed in Black newspapers after emancipation by people searching for relatives sold decades earlier. Those advertisements are the trade's own casualty list, compiled by the victims, and they ran for forty years.

What falls between the recorded events, and is counted nowhere.

Transmitted harm documented

Descent lines severed at the point of sale. A person sold from Maryland to Louisiana appears in two counties' records under two owners and often two names, so the genealogical thread breaks exactly where the trade cut it - which is why descendants today frequently cannot document the ancestry that a claim would require of them.

Established by record: title transferred, population displaced, wealth destroyed, measured effects.

Respondents

Slave-trading firms, among them Franklin & Armfield of Alexandria, the largest in the trade; the sellers and buyers named in every bill of sale; the county clerks who recorded them; the banks that took enslaved people as collateral; the insurers who underwrote their lives; and the Customs Service, which required and retained a manifest for every person shipped coastwise.

Process and disposition

No forum, because the trade was lawful and its participants were engaged in commerce the law protected. The federal government did not prohibit it; it regulated it, requiring a manifest naming every person shipped coastwise and preserving those manifests in the customs records. That is the characteristic fact of this entry: the state's involvement was administrative rather than prosecutorial, and the paperwork it demanded is now the evidence.

Route of authority

The actor stood at private holder. Each rung the matter reached, or failed to.

  1. private holder Buyer, seller, and trading firm no process
    The transaction was the conduct. There was no complaint to bring.
  2. county County deed and probate books no process
    The county recorded the sale to make title good. It was the registrar of the conduct, not a forum for complaint about it.
  3. federal U.S. Customs Service, coastwise manifests required from 1808 no process
    Federal law required the manifest and the federal government kept it. The only rung of the ladder that touched this trade did so to document it.

Bills of sale, deed books, firm ledgers, coastwise manifests and insurance policies establish the transactions; the WPA narratives, the post-emancipation search advertisements, and narratives such as Northup's establish what the transactions did to the people inside them.

Entered separately from chattel enslavement because the trade is a distinct course of conduct with a distinct and unusually complete documentary record. Roughly 110 people were taken from Ouidah and carried to Mobile Bay in 1860, more than fifty years after the United States prohibited the international slave trade and forty years after Congress declared participation in it piracy punishable by death. The vessel was burned on arrival to destroy the evidence. The survivors were distributed among the financiers and, after emancipation, founded Africatown near Mobile.

Kossola, known as Cudjo Lewis, Matilda McCrear, The people carried on the Clotilda

Respondents

Timothy Meaher, who financed the voyage on a wager, and William Foster, who captained the vessel and burned it

Process and disposition

Forum: United States District Court, Southern District of Alabama

This is the entry in which the conduct was unambiguously a crime under United States law at the time it was committed - a capital one - and still produced no punishment. Federal proceedings were commenced and came to nothing; the outbreak of war ended them. The wreck was located and confirmed by the Alabama Historical Commission in 2019, and the descendants of the people carried on it and of the man who financed the voyage both still live in Mobile.
